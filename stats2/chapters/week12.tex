\section{Student's $t$-Test for Unknown Variance}

When the population variance $\sigma^2$ is unknown, substituting the unbiased sample variance $S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2$ into the standard normal pivot introduces extra random variation.

\begin{intuitionbox}{Origin of Student's $t$-Distribution}
In 1908, William Sealy Gosset (publishing under the pseudonym "Student" while working at the Guinness brewery) analyzed small-sample chemical quality tests. Gosset showed that dividing a normal variable by an independent estimate of its standard deviation produces a distribution with heavier tails than the standard normal distribution:
$$T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V / \nu}} \sim t_{\nu}$$
where $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $V \sim \chi_{\nu}^2$ are independent random variables, and $\nu = n-1$ denotes degrees of freedom. As sample size $n \to \infty$, $S \xrightarrow{P} \sigma$ by the Law of Large Numbers, and $t_{n-1} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{definitionbox}{One-Sample $t$-Test Statistic}
\index{One-Sample t-Test}
To test $H_0 \colon \mu = \mu_0$ versus $H_1 \colon \mu \neq \mu_0$ from an i.i.d. normal sample $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with unknown $\sigma^2$:
$$T_{\text{calc}} = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu_0}{S / \sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}$$
where the standard error is $\text{SE}(\bar{X}) = \frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}$. At significance level $\alpha$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1 \colon \mu \neq \mu_0$ if $|T_{\text{calc}}| \ge t_{\alpha/2, n-1}$.
    \item Reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1 \colon \mu > \mu_0$ if $T_{\text{calc}} \ge t_{\alpha, n-1}$.
    \item Reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1 \colon \mu < \mu_0$ if $T_{\text{calc}} \le -t_{\alpha, n-1}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\section{Two-Sample and Paired Tests}

In A/B testing and experimental evaluation, we compare treatment and control groups under two distinct sampling designs: independent samples versus repeated/matched measurements.

\begin{formulabox}{Independent Two-Sample $t$-Tests}
\index{Two-Sample t-Test}
Let $X_1, \dots, X_{n_1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $Y_1, \dots, Y_{n_2} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ be independent samples. We test $H_0 \colon \mu_1 - \mu_2 = \Delta_0$ (typically $\Delta_0 = 0$).

\textbf{1. Equal Variances Assumed ($\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2$): Pooled $t$-Test}
The pooled sample variance is the degree-of-freedom weighted average:
$$S_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)S_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)S_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}$$
The test statistic is:
$$T_{\text{calc}} = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \Delta_0}{S_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}} \sim t_{n_1 + n_2 - 2}$$

\textbf{2. Unequal Variances ($\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2$): Welch's $t$-Test}
When equal variance cannot be assumed, Welch's unpooled test statistic is:
$$T_{\text{Welch}} = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \Delta_0}{\sqrt{\frac{S_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{S_2^2}{n_2}}} \sim t_{\nu_{\text{Welch}}}$$
with degrees of freedom approximated by the Welch-Satterthwaite equation:
$$\nu_{\text{Welch}} = \frac{\left(\frac{S_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{S_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\frac{(S_1^2 / n_1)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \frac{(S_2^2 / n_2)^2}{n_2 - 1}}$$
\end{formulabox}

\begin{formulabox}{Paired Differences $t$-Test}
\index{Paired t-Test}
When subjects are measured before and after a treatment (or paired in twin/matched designs), observations $(X_i, Y_i)$ are dependent. We compute unit-level differences:
$$D_i = X_i - Y_i \quad \text{for } i = 1, \dots, n$$
This collapses the problem into a \textbf{one-sample $t$-test} on $D$:
$$T_{\text{paired}} = \frac{\bar{D} - \Delta_0}{S_D / \sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}$$
where $\bar{D} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n D_i$ and $S_D = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (D_i - \bar{D})^2}$.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{trapbox}{Mistake: Running Independent $t$-Test on Paired Experimental Data}
Applying an independent two-sample $t$-test to before/after or matched data is a severe methodological blunder. Because subjects are positively correlated across time ($\text{Cov}(X, Y) > 0$), the variance of the difference satisfies:
$$\Var(X - Y) = \Var(X) + \Var(Y) - 2\Cov(X, Y)$$
An independent test ignores the covariance term $-2\Cov(X, Y)$, substantially overestimating the standard error, artificially inflating the denominator of $T$, and destroying statistical power!
\end{trapbox}

\section{The Neyman-Pearson Lemma}

How do we construct the mathematically optimal test that achieves the maximum possible power $1 - \beta$ for a fixed significance level $\alpha$?

\begin{definitionbox}{The Neyman-Pearson Lemma}
\index{Neyman-Pearson Lemma}
Consider testing a simple null hypothesis against a simple alternative hypothesis:
$$H_0 \colon \theta = \theta_0 \quad \text{versus} \quad H_1 \colon \theta = \theta_1$$
based on sample vector $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_n)$ with joint likelihood $L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})$.
The likelihood ratio is:
$$\lambda(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{L(\theta_0 \mid \mathbf{x})}{L(\theta_1 \mid \mathbf{x})}$$
The decision rule that defines rejection region:
$$R = \{\mathbf{x} \colon \lambda(\mathbf{x}) \le k\} = \left\{\mathbf{x} \colon \frac{L(\theta_1 \mid \mathbf{x})}{L(\theta_0 \mid \mathbf{x})} \ge c\right\}$$
where $k$ (or $c = 1/k$) is chosen such that $\P(\mathbf{X} \in R \mid \theta = \theta_0) = \alpha$, is the \textbf{Most Powerful (MP) test of size $\alpha$}. Any other test with size $\alpha' \le \alpha$ has power strictly less than or equal to this likelihood ratio test.
\end{definitionbox}

\section{The Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) \& Wilks' Theorem}

When hypotheses are composite (e.g. parameter sets $\Theta_0 \subset \Theta$), the Neyman-Pearson ratio generalizes to the supremum over parameter spaces.

\begin{definitionbox}{Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT)}
\index{Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT)}
Let $\mathbf{X} \sim f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta)$ with $\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p$. We test nested hypotheses:
$$H_0 \colon \theta \in \Theta_0 \quad \text{versus} \quad H_1 \colon \theta \in \Theta \setminus \Theta_0$$
The generalized likelihood ratio statistic is:
$$\Lambda(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})}{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta} L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})} = \frac{L(\hat{\theta}_0 \mid \mathbf{x})}{L(\hat{\theta} \mid \mathbf{x})}$$
where $\hat{\theta}_0$ is the constrained MLE under $H_0$, and $\hat{\theta}$ is the unconstrained global MLE over the full space $\Theta$.
Because $\Theta_0 \subseteq \Theta$, the maximum over $\Theta$ must be at least as large as the maximum over $\Theta_0$:
$$0 \le \Lambda(\mathbf{x}) \le 1$$
Values of $\Lambda(\mathbf{x})$ close to 1 favor $H_0$; values close to 0 indicate that the data is much more probable under the unconstrained alternative.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{formulabox}{Wilks' Theorem (Asymptotic Distribution of LRT)}
\index{Wilks' Theorem}
Under standard Fisher regularity conditions (smooth log-likelihood, true parameter in the interior of the parameter space), as sample size $n \to \infty$, under $H_0$:
$$W(\mathbf{x}) = -2 \ln \Lambda(\mathbf{x}) = 2 \left[\ln L(\hat{\theta}) - \ln L(\hat{\theta}_0)\right] \xrightarrow{d} \chi_r^2$$
where the degrees of freedom $r$ equals the difference in dimensionality:
$$r = \dim(\Theta) - \dim(\Theta_0) = \text{number of free parameters restricted by } H_0$$
At significance level $\alpha$, the LRT decision rule rejects $H_0$ if:
$$W(\mathbf{x}) = -2 \ln \Lambda(\mathbf{x}) \ge \chi_{\alpha, r}^2$$
\end{formulabox}

\begin{trapbox}{Degrees of Freedom Count Parameters Restricted, NOT Sample Size}
A frequent exam blunder is setting the degrees of freedom $r$ in Wilks' theorem to $n-1$ or $n-k$. The asymptotic distribution $\chi_r^2$ is determined \textbf{strictly by the number of independent constraints} imposed on the parameter vector by $H_0$:
$$r = \dim(\Theta) - \dim(\Theta_0)$$
For instance, in testing whether $k$ regression coefficients are simultaneously zero in a model with sample size $n = 100{,}000$, $r = k$, completely independent of $n$!
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Regularity Condition Breakdown: Parameters on the Boundary}
Wilks' theorem fundamentally assumes that the null parameter vector $\theta_0$ lies in the \textbf{interior} of the parameter space $\Theta$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Boundary Failure:} If we test whether a variance component is zero ($H_0 \colon \sigma^2 = 0$ vs. $H_1 \colon \sigma^2 > 0$), or whether a mixture weight is zero ($H_0 \colon w = 0$), the true value sits directly on the boundary of the feasible parameter space $[0, \infty)$ or $[0, 1]$.
    \item \textbf{Consequence:} The score function derivative cannot be symmetric, the Taylor expansion breaks, and $-2 \ln \Lambda$ does \textbf{not} follow a $\chi_1^2$ distribution. Instead, it asymptotically follows an equal mixture:
    $$-2 \ln \Lambda \xrightarrow{d} \frac{1}{2}\chi_0^2 + \frac{1}{2}\chi_1^2$$
    Using the naive $\chi_1^2$ critical value yields an overly conservative test with drastically reduced power!
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\section{The Trinity of Testing: Wald, Score (Rao), and LRT}

In statistical theory and econometric machine learning, three asymptotically equivalent classical testing paradigms form the "Holy Trinity" of hypothesis testing.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.2cm}p{3.8cm}p{4.2cm}p{3.0cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Test} & \textbf{Estimation Required} & \textbf{Geometric / Mathematical Concept} & \textbf{Reparameterization Invariance} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Wald Test} & Unconstrained $\hat{\theta}$ only & Evaluates quadratic distance $(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)^T I(\hat{\theta})(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)$ & \textbf{Not invariant} to nonlinear transforms \\
\textbf{Score (Rao) Test} & Constrained $\hat{\theta}_0$ only & Evaluates slope/gradient (score) $S(\theta_0)$ and Fisher information at null & \textbf{Invariant} \\
\textbf{Likelihood Ratio (LRT)} & Both unconstrained $\hat{\theta}$ and constrained $\hat{\theta}_0$ & Evaluates vertical drop $2[\ell(\hat{\theta}) - \ell(\hat{\theta}_0)]$ in log-likelihood & \textbf{Strictly Invariant} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{intuitionbox}{When to Use Which Test in Machine Learning}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Score (Rao) Test:} Ideal when fitting the unconstrained model is computationally prohibitive (e.g. testing whether 100,000 new parameters are zero in a massive network). You only fit the restricted null model, compute the gradient with respect to the new parameters at zero, and test if the gradient deviates from zero.
    \item \textbf{Wald Test:} Ideal when the complex unconstrained model is already fitted (e.g. full linear or logistic regression), and you wish to test individual coefficient significance without refitting.
    \item \textbf{LRT:} The gold standard for statistical power and invariant inference whenever fitting both models is computationally feasible.
\end{itemize}
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Pearson's Chi-Squared ($\chi^2$) Tests}

Pearson's $\chi^2$ test assesses whether multinomial count frequencies conform to expected probabilities or whether two categorical variables are independent.

\begin{formulabox}{Pearson's Goodness-of-Fit Test}
\index{Chi-Squared Goodness of Fit}
Let sample size $N$ be partitioned into $k$ mutually exclusive categories with observed counts $O_1, \dots, O_k$ such that $\sum_{i=1}^k O_i = N$. Under $H_0$, the theoretical probabilities are $p_1(\theta), \dots, p_k(\theta)$, yielding expected cell counts:
$$E_i = N \cdot p_i(\theta)$$
Pearson's test statistic is:
$$\chi_{\text{calc}}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i} \xrightarrow{d} \chi_{df}^2$$
where the degrees of freedom are:
$$df = k - 1 - m$$
with $k$ being the number of categories, $1$ subtracted because $\sum O_i = N$ is fixed, and $m$ being the number of unknown parameters estimated from the sample via MLE.
\textbf{Rule of Thumb:} For asymptotic validity, all expected counts should satisfy $E_i \ge 5$.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{formulabox}{Chi-Squared Test of Independence (Contingency Tables)}
\index{Chi-Squared Test of Independence}
To test whether two categorical factors (Row with $R$ levels, Column with $C$ levels) are independent:
$$H_0 \colon p_{ij} = p_{i\cdot} \cdot p_{\cdot j} \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_1 \colon p_{ij} \neq p_{i\cdot} \cdot p_{\cdot j}$$
Given total observations $N = \sum_{i=1}^R \sum_{j=1}^C O_{ij}$, row sums $R_i = \sum_{j=1}^C O_{ij}$, and column sums $C_j = \sum_{i=1}^R O_{ij}$:
$$E_{ij} = \frac{R_i \cdot C_j}{N}$$
$$\chi_{\text{calc}}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^R \sum_{j=1}^C \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} \sim \chi_{(R - 1)(C - 1)}^2$$
Reject independence if $\chi_{\text{calc}}^2 \ge \chi_{\alpha, (R-1)(C-1)}^2$.
\end{formulabox}

\section{Python Implementation: $t$-Tests, Goodness-of-Fit, and LRT}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={SciPy and Statsmodels Implementation of Advanced Testing}]
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

# 1. One-Sample t-Test (H0: mu = 100)
sample = np.array([102, 105, 98, 108, 101, 104, 99, 106, 103, 100, 107, 102, 105, 101, 104, 98])
t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_1samp(sample, popmean=100)
print(f"One-Sample t-test: t = {t_stat:.3f}, p = {p_val:.4f}, df = {len(sample)-1}")

# 2. Independent Two-Sample Pooled t-Test
g1 = [15, 17, 14, 16, 15, 18, 14, 16, 15, 15]
g2 = [12, 13, 11, 14, 12, 13, 10, 12, 13, 12]
t_pooled, p_pooled = stats.ttest_ind(g1, g2, equal_var=True)
print(f"Pooled Two-Sample t-test: t = {t_pooled:.3f}, p = {p_pooled:.4e}")

# 3. Welch's Unpooled t-Test (Unequal Variances)
t_welch, p_welch = stats.ttest_ind(g1, g2, equal_var=False)
print(f"Welch's t-test: t = {t_welch:.3f}, p = {p_welch:.4e}")

# 4. Pearson's Chi-Squared Goodness of Fit (6-sided Fair Die)
observed = np.array([15, 7, 8, 12, 10, 8])
expected = np.array([10, 10, 10, 10, 10, 10])
chi2_stat, p_chi2 = stats.chisquare(f_obs=observed, f_exp=expected)
print(f"Chi2 Goodness-of-Fit: chi2 = {chi2_stat:.3f}, p = {p_chi2:.4f}, df = {len(observed)-1}")

# 5. Chi-Squared Contingency Table Test of Independence
table = np.array([[80, 40],   # Free Tier: [Retained, Churned]
                  [70, 10]])  # Premium Tier: [Retained, Churned]
chi2_tab, p_tab, dof, ex = stats.chi2_contingency(table, correction=False)
print(f"Contingency Test: chi2 = {chi2_tab:.3f}, p = {p_tab:.4f}, dof = {dof}")
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Part B: Conceptual and Diagnostic Multiple Choice Questions}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: The Trinity of Statistical Testing in High Dimensions} \\
A machine learning engineer is training a large generalized linear model with $p = 100{,}000$ candidate feature weights $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. The engineering team wants to test the null hypothesis that a block of $r = 500$ newly engineered features has zero effect:
$$H_0 \colon \theta_{p-r+1} = \dots = \theta_p = 0$$
Fitting the full, unconstrained model requires 24 hours of GPU cluster time, whereas the baseline model under $H_0$ is already converged and fitted. Which testing procedure should the team employ, and what is its theoretical justification?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wald Test}, because it only requires evaluating the Fisher information and log-likelihood at the null hypothesis $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$.
    \item \textbf{Score (Rao) Test}, because it evaluates the gradient (score vector) and curvature of the log-likelihood strictly at the constrained null estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$, requiring zero optimization steps on the unconstrained model.
    \item \textbf{Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT)}, because it automatically avoids fitting the null model by using an unconstrained Taylor series approximation.
    \item \textbf{Wald Test}, because the Wald test statistic is strictly invariant to nonlinear coordinate transformations of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: Degrees of Freedom in Goodness-of-Fit and Wilks' Theorem} \\
A data scientist observes $N = 5{,}000$ customer service calls classified into $K = 6$ time-of-day bins: $[0, 4), [4, 8), [8, 12), [12, 16), [16, 20), [20, 24)$. To test whether arrivals follow an inhomogeneous Poisson process governed by a 2-parameter diurnal intensity function $\lambda(t; \alpha, \beta)$, the scientist estimates $\hat{\alpha}$ and $\hat{\beta}$ by maximum likelihood from the sample, computes expected bin counts $E_1, \dots, E_6$, and calculates Pearson's $\chi_{\text{calc}}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^6 \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$. Under $H_0$, what is the asymptotic distribution of $\chi_{\text{calc}}^2$?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\chi^2$ with $N - 1 = 4{,}999$ degrees of freedom.
    \item $\chi^2$ with $K - 1 = 6 - 1 = 5$ degrees of freedom.
    \item $\chi^2$ with $K - 1 - m = 6 - 1 - 2 = 3$ degrees of freedom.
    \item $\chi^2$ with $K - m = 6 - 2 = 4$ degrees of freedom.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
