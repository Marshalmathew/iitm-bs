\section*{Week 1: Introduction and Types of Data}

\subsection*{Detailed Solutions}

\textbf{1. Concepts: Identify the sample, population, statistic, parameter, descriptive, and inferential parts.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sample:} The 500 patients in the trial.
    \item \textbf{Population:} The general population of all people with high blood pressure.
    \item \textbf{Statistic:} The 12-point average drop in blood pressure (calculated exactly from the sample).
    \item \textbf{Parameter:} The true average drop in blood pressure for the entire population (which is unknown).
    \item \textbf{Descriptive Part:} The act of calculating and reporting the 12-point drop in the 500 patients.
    \item \textbf{Inferential Part:} The act of generalizing that result to conclude the drug works for everyone.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2. Data Classification: Classify the variables.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(a) Emails received:} Numerical Discrete (you can count them: 0, 1, 2, but you can't get 1.5 emails).
    \item \textbf{(b) Letter grades (A, B, C, D, F):} Categorical Ordinal (they are categories, but A is definitively better than B).
    \item \textbf{(c) Exact volume of water:} Numerical Continuous (it can be exactly 500.1234 ml, falling anywhere on the interval).
    \item \textbf{(d) Laptop brand:} Categorical Nominal (categories with no natural rank or order).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3. Scales of Measurement: Identify the scale.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(a) Manufacturing\_Year:} \textbf{Interval}. Gaps are equal (2018 - 2015 = 3 years), but there is no absolute zero (Year 0 doesn't mean "no time").
    \item \textbf{(b) Mileage\_Driven:} \textbf{Ratio}. A car with 0 km has truly been driven "zero distance."
    \item \textbf{(c) Color:} \textbf{Nominal}. Unordered labels.
    \item \textbf{(d) Condition\_Rating (1-Poor, 2-Fair, 3-Excellent):} \textbf{Ordinal}. The numbers imply order, but the gap between Poor and Fair isn't mathematically identical to Fair and Excellent.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{4. Data Structures: Time Series vs. Cross-Sectional.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Patient X over a week:} \textbf{Time Series}. It observes a single subject across multiple time points.
    \item \textbf{100 patients on Monday morning:} \textbf{Cross-Sectional}. It observes multiple subjects at a single snapshot in time.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{5. Data Science Application: Feature Engineering for Postal Pincodes}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A postal PIN code is a \textbf{Categorical Nominal} identifier encoded numerically.
    \item If fed into a linear model as an unscaled continuous numerical feature, the model interprets the magnitudes arithmetically: e.g. predicting that PIN 600036 has roughly double the additive effect of PIN 300018. This imposes an arbitrary, fictitious spatial ordering that distorts optimization and leads to catastrophic generalization error.
    \item \textbf{Proper Encoding:} Postal codes should be modeled as discrete nominal categorical variables using \textbf{Target Encoding}, \textbf{Entity Embeddings}, or clustered spatial coordinates (latitude and longitude).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{6. Conceptual Diagnostic: Scale Invariance and Meaningful Operations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (c)}
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is correct:} On an \textbf{Interval} scale, the zero point is arbitrary, so individual values undergo an affine transformation $y = ax + b$ ($a > 0$). While absolute ratios are not preserved:
    $$\frac{F_2}{F_1} = \frac{\frac{9}{5}C_2 + 32}{\frac{9}{5}C_1 + 32} \neq \frac{C_2}{C_1}$$
    the ratio of differences is strictly invariant:
    $$\frac{F_3 - F_1}{F_2 - F_1} = \frac{\frac{9}{5}(C_3 - C_1)}{\frac{9}{5}(C_2 - C_1)} = \frac{C_3 - C_1}{C_2 - C_1}$$
    This invariance of difference ratios is the mathematical defining property of Stevens' Interval scale.
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is false:} $0^\circ\text{C}$ is the freezing point of water at 1 atm, not absolute zero (which is $-273.15^\circ\text{C} = 0\,\text{K}$).
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is false:} In Celsius, $20/10 = 2$. In Fahrenheit, $10^\circ\text{C} = 50^\circ\text{F}$ and $20^\circ\text{C} = 68^\circ\text{F}$, giving $68/50 = 1.36 \neq 2$. Ratios are mathematically meaningless on interval scales.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} Adding a constant shift $+32$ shifts the baseline but does not alter the equality of intervals ($\Delta F = \frac{9}{5}\Delta C$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{7. Conceptual Diagnostic: Parameter vs. Statistic in Model Validation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (a)}
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is correct:} The average $\bar{x} = 24.2\,\text{m}$ is a \textbf{sample statistic} calculated from a finite sample of $n = 1{,}000$ test cases. The true parameter $\mu$ is an unobserved, fixed constant representing the expected value across the entire infinite population of possible scenarios. Due to random sampling variation, $\bar{x} \neq \mu$ in general; rather, $\bar{x}$ is a point estimate subject to a standard error $\text{SE} = s/\sqrt{n} = 3.1/\sqrt{1000} \approx 0.098\,\text{m}$.
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is false:} Stopping distance possesses a true physical zero ($0\,\text{metres}$ means no motion) and is a legitimate \textbf{Ratio} scale.
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} Distance can take any continuous real value within an interval; it is continuous.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} Point estimation is a parametric procedure; the mistake is failing to recognize sampling error.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{1. Frequency and Relative Frequency Calculations}

\begin{itemize}

\textbf{2. Pareto Analysis in Quality Engineering}
Total downtime incidents $n = 1{,}000$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Percentages and Cumulative Percentages:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Network Outage: $f = 480 \implies 48.0\% \implies \text{Cumulative} = \mathbf{48.0\%}$.
        \item Database Deadlock: $f = 290 \implies 29.0\% \implies \text{Cumulative} = 48.0 + 29.0 = \mathbf{77.0\%}$.
        \item Software Bug: $f = 130 \implies 13.0\% \implies \text{Cumulative} = 77.0 + 13.0 = \mathbf{90.0\%}$.
        \item Hardware Fault: $f = 70 \implies 7.0\% \implies \text{Cumulative} = 90.0 + 7.0 = \mathbf{97.0\%}$.
        \item Operator Error: $f = 30 \implies 3.0\% \implies \text{Cumulative} = 97.0 + 3.0 = \mathbf{100.0\%}$.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vital Few:} Network Outages ($48\%$) and Database Deadlocks ($29\%$) together account for $77.0\%$ of all downtime events. Remedying these two root causes resolves the vast majority ($\ge 75\%$) of system failures.
\end{enumerate}

Total sample $n = 400$:
\begin{enumerate}

    $$P(\text{Purchased}) = \frac{\text{Total Purchased}}{n} = \frac{140}{400} = \mathbf{0.350 \quad (35.0\%)}$$

\textbf{4. Ordinal Median Determination}
Sample size $n = 80$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mode:} The category with the highest frequency count ($f = 27$) is \textbf{Agree (4)}.
    \item \textbf{Cumulative Counts:}

        \item Disagree (2): $6 + 14 = 20$

    \item For $n = 80$, the median lies between the 40th and 41st ranked observations. Since the cumulative count reaches 45 at Neutral, both the 40th and 41st observations fall strictly inside \textbf{Neutral (3)}.
    $$\text{Median Category} = \mathbf{\text{Neutral (3)}}$$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{5. Graphing Ethics Analysis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual Height Ratio:} With baseline starting at $\$10.0\text{M}$:

    $$\text{Screen Height Ratio} = \frac{0.8}{0.2} = \mathbf{4.0} \quad (\text{Q2 appears } 300\% \text{ taller than Q1})$$

    Truncating the y-axis creates an optical distortion that exaggerates a modest $5.88\%$ increase into a perceived $400\%$ height ratio.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{6. Conceptual Diagnostic: Mechanism of Simpson's Paradox}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (b)}
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is correct:} Simpson's Paradox is driven by an unobserved or ignored confounding variable (patient case severity) that is strongly correlated with both treatment choice and survival probability. Even though Procedure A has higher survival in both mild ($95\% > 90\%$) and severe ($70\% > 60\%$) cohorts, Procedure A was assigned disproportionately to high-risk severe cases (where survival is low across all procedures), while Procedure B was predominantly reserved for low-risk mild cases. When pooled, the heavier weighting of severe cases drags Procedure A's overall marginal rate below Procedure B's.
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is false:} Percentages can be aggregated if weighted properly by cohort sample sizes; the paradox is caused by confounding allocation, not arithmetic addition errors.
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} Success/failure is a binary categorical outcome, not a continuous ratio variable.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} The law of total probability holds perfectly; it is precisely the unequal conditional allocation weights in the law of total probability that generates the reversal.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{7. Conceptual Diagnostic: Central Tendency for Ordinal Categorical Distributions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (b)}
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is correct:} Ordinal data possesses rank order but lacks equidistant intervals between categories. Mapping ordinal labels to arbitrary integers ($1, 2, 3, 4$) falsely assumes equal intervals, making the calculated mean $\bar{x} = 2.51$ mathematically uninterpretable. The proper, scale-invariant measure of central tendency is the median. For $n = 101$, the median observation is ranked $(101 + 1)/2 = 51^{\text{st}}$. Cumulative frequencies: $U$ has $20$, $N$ reaches $20 + 25 = 45$, and $S$ reaches $45 + 40 = 85$. The $51^{\text{st}}$ employee falls squarely into \textbf{Satisfied ($S$)}.
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is false:} Arbitrary integer coding does not transform an ordinal scale into an interval scale; the true psychological spacing remains non-uniform.
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} The mode is Satisfied ($S$) with count 40 (not Neutral with count 25), and medians are fully defined for ordinal data.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} A median on an ordinal scale should be expressed as the category label itself, not an arbitrary numerical score.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Week 3: Describing Numerical Data: Central Tendency}

\subsection*{Detailed Solutions}

\textbf{1. Outlier Sensitivity and Skewness}
Given compensation dataset in INR: $\{70k, 75k, 80k, 80k, 85k, 90k, 1480k\}$ with $n = 7$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mean and Median:}
    $$\text{Sum} = 70 + 75 + 80 + 80 + 85 + 90 + 1{,}480 = 1{,}960\,\text{thousand}$$
    $$\bar{x} = \frac{1{,}960{,}000}{7} = \mathbf{280{,}000\,\text{INR}}$$
    For sorted sample, the middle observation ($4^{\text{th}}$ position) is $\tilde{x} = \mathbf{80{,}000\,\text{INR}}$.
    \item \textbf{14.3\% Trimmed Mean:} Discarding the single lowest ($70k$) and single highest ($1480k$) leaves 5 values: $\{75k, 80k, 80k, 85k, 90k\}$.
    $$\bar{x}_{0.143} = \frac{75 + 80 + 80 + 85 + 90}{5} = \frac{410{,}000}{5} = \mathbf{82{,}000\,\text{INR}}$$
    \item \textbf{Skewness:} Because $\bar{x} = 280{,}000 \gg 80{,}000 = \tilde{x}$, the single executive compensation pulls the arithmetic mean heavily to the right, producing extreme \textbf{Right-Skewness} (positive skew).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{2. The Zero-Sum Deviation Property}
For $X = \{12, 15, 18, 22, 28\}$ ($n = 5$):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample Mean:}
    $$\bar{x} = \frac{12 + 15 + 18 + 22 + 28}{5} = \frac{95}{5} = \mathbf{19.0}$$
    \item \textbf{Verification of Deviations:}
    \begin{align*}
        x_1 - \bar{x} &= 12 - 19 = -7 \\
        x_2 - \bar{x} &= 15 - 19 = -4 \\
        x_3 - \bar{x} &= 18 - 19 = -1 \\
        x_4 - \bar{x} &= 22 - 19 = +3 \\
        x_5 - \bar{x} &= 28 - 19 = +9
    \end{align*}
    $$\sum_{i=1}^5 (x_i - \bar{x}) = (-7) + (-4) + (-1) + 3 + 9 = -12 + 12 = \mathbf{0}$$
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{3. Weighted Mean of Aggregated Batches}
Given shift sample sizes and means:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Shift A: $w_1 = 500, \bar{x}_1 = 12.4\,\text{mm} \implies \text{Total} = 500 \times 12.4 = 6{,}200\,\text{mm}$.
    \item Shift B: $w_2 = 300, \bar{x}_2 = 12.8\,\text{mm} \implies \text{Total} = 300 \times 12.8 = 3{,}840\,\text{mm}$.
    \item Shift C: $w_3 = 200, \bar{x}_3 = 13.5\,\text{mm} \implies \text{Total} = 200 \times 13.5 = 2{,}700\,\text{mm}$.
\end{itemize}
$$\bar{x}_{\text{combined}} = \frac{6{,}200 + 3{,}840 + 2{,}700}{500 + 300 + 200} = \frac{12{,}740}{1{,}000} = \mathbf{12.74\,\text{mm}}$$

\textbf{4. Optimization Property Verification}
Given $\{2, 5, 11\}$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Minimizing $L_2$ Loss $\sum (x_i - c)^2$:}
    $$c = \bar{x} = \frac{2 + 5 + 11}{3} = \frac{18}{3} = \mathbf{6.0}$$
    Check: at $c=6$, $\sum(x_i - 6)^2 = 16 + 1 + 25 = 42$. (At $c=5$, loss is $9 + 0 + 36 = 45 > 42$).
    \item \textbf{Minimizing $L_1$ Loss $\sum |x_i - c|$:}
    $$c = \tilde{x} = \text{Median} = \mathbf{5.0}$$
    Check: at $c=5$, $\sum|x_i - 5| = 3 + 0 + 6 = 9$. (At $c=6$, loss is $4 + 1 + 5 = 10 > 9$).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{5. Histogram Binning Impact}
Sample $n = 100$, range $R = 92 - 12 = 80\,\text{ms}$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Recommended number of bins: $k = \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil = \lceil\sqrt{100}\rceil = \mathbf{10}$ bins.
    \item Class interval bin width: $w = \frac{R}{k} = \frac{80}{10} = \mathbf{8.0\,\text{ms}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{6. Conceptual Diagnostic: Loss Functions and Model Invariance}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (b)}
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is correct:} The sample median is the unique mathematical minimizer of the sum of absolute deviations $\sum |y_i - c|$. In machine learning regression, training with Mean Absolute Error (MAE / $L_1$ loss) outputs the conditional median $\text{Median}(Y \mid X)$. The median is completely immune to the numerical magnitude of extreme outliers (possessing a $50\%$ breakdown point), making it the optimal statistical objective for heavy-tailed financial loss modeling.
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is false:} MSE loss estimates the arithmetic mean, which is pulled arbitrarily far by multi-million-dollar default losses, severely biasing typical predictions.
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} MAPE divides by actual $y$, exploding to infinity or becoming undefined when $y = 0$.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} The mode does not minimize continuous $L_1$ or $L_2$ loss; it represents peak density.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{7. Conceptual Diagnostic: Aggregation of Group Averages}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (a)}
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is correct:} The unweighted arithmetic mean of group averages treats each subsidiary as if it had the exact same number of employees. The true conglomerate average requires weighting by headcount:
    $$\text{Total Payroll} = 100(\$120{,}000) + 300(\$80{,}000) + 600(\$50{,}000) = \$12\text{M} + \$24\text{M} + \$30\text{M} = \$66{,}000{,}000$$
    $$\bar{x}_{\text{true}} = \frac{\$66{,}000{,}000}{100 + 300 + 600} = \frac{\$66{,}000{,}000}{1{,}000} = \mathbf{\$66{,}000}$$
    The officer's calculation overestimated the corporate average by over $\$17{,}000$ by over-weighting small, high-paying Alpha ($100$ staff) and under-weighting large, lower-paying Gamma ($600$ staff).
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is false:} Group membership does not erase unequal sample size weights.
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} Gamma's mean does not override others; all $1{,}000$ employees contribute to the weighted numerator.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} The median of group means is not the overall mean.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Week 4: Association Between Two Variables: Covariance \& Correlation}

\subsection*{Detailed Solutions}

\textbf{1. Contingency Tables: Housing vs. Commute}
Sample size $n = 300$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Marginal Distribution of Commuting Mode:}
    $$P(\text{Walk}) = \frac{100}{300} \approx \mathbf{33.3\%}, \quad P(\text{Bike}) = \frac{60}{300} = \mathbf{20.0\%}, \quad P(\text{Transit}) = \frac{140}{300} \approx \mathbf{46.7\%}$$
    \item \textbf{Conditional Distribution Given On-Campus ($n_{\text{on}} = 110$):}
    $$P(\text{Walk} \mid \text{On}) = \frac{80}{110} \approx \mathbf{72.7\%}, \quad P(\text{Bike} \mid \text{On}) = \frac{20}{110} \approx \mathbf{18.2\%}, \quad P(\text{Transit} \mid \text{On}) = \frac{10}{110} \approx \mathbf{9.1\%}$$
    \item \textbf{Independence Assessment:} For Off-Campus students, $P(\text{Transit} \mid \text{Off}) = \frac{130}{190} \approx 68.4\% \neq 9.1\%$. Because the conditional distributions differ substantially across residence categories, Housing and Commuting Mode are \textbf{statistically dependent (associated)}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{2. Pearson's $r$ Step-by-Step Calculation}
Given $X = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ and $Y = \{2, 4, 5, 4, 5\}$ with $n = 5$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample Means:}
    $$\bar{x} = \frac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5}{5} = \mathbf{3.0}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{2 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 5}{5} = \mathbf{4.0}$$
    \item \textbf{Sample Covariance ($s_{xy}$):}
    $$\sum_{i=1}^5 x_i y_i = (1)(2) + (2)(4) + (3)(5) + (4)(4) + (5)(5) = 2 + 8 + 15 + 16 + 25 = 66$$
    $$s_{xy} = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum x_i y_i - n\bar{x}\bar{y}\right) = \frac{1}{4}(66 - 5(3.0)(4.0)) = \frac{66 - 60}{4} = \frac{6}{4} = \mathbf{1.50}$$
    \item \textbf{Standard Deviations:}
    $$\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = (-2)^2 + (-1)^2 + 0^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10 \implies s_x = \sqrt{\frac{10}{4}} = \sqrt{2.5} \approx 1.581$$
    $$\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = (-2)^2 + 0^2 + 1^2 + 0^2 + 1^2 = 4 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 1 = 6 \implies s_y = \sqrt{\frac{6}{4}} = \sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.225$$
    \item \textbf{Pearson's $r$:}
    $$r = \frac{s_{xy}}{s_x s_y} = \frac{1.50}{\sqrt{2.5}\sqrt{1.5}} = \frac{1.50}{\sqrt{3.75}} = \frac{1.50}{1.9365} \approx \mathbf{0.7746}$$
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{3. Monotonic Non-Linear Association ($Y = X^3$)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spearman's $\rho_s$:} Because $f(x) = x^3$ is a \emph{strictly monotonic increasing function}, the rank orders of $X$ and $Y$ are identical ($R(x_i) = R(y_i)$ for all $i$). Thus, $d_i = 0$ for all $i$, yielding $\rho_s = \mathbf{1.000}$.
    \item \textbf{Pearson's $r < 1.00$:} Pearson's $r$ measures strictly linear alignment ($y = ax + b$). Because the cubic curve bends upward with increasing slope, the points cannot lie on any straight line, guaranteeing $r < 1.00$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{4. Multicollinearity in Machine Learning}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Correlation:} Weight in pounds is an exact positive linear scaling of weight in kilograms ($W_{\text{lbs}} = 2.20462 \cdot W_{\text{kg}}$). By the affine invariance theorem with $a = 2.20462 > 0$, the correlation is \textbf{exactly $r = 1.000$}.
    \item \textbf{Matrix Catastrophe:} In multiple linear regression, the parameter vector is estimated via $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}$. Perfect multicollinearity ($r = 1$) makes two columns of matrix $\mathbf{X}$ linearly dependent, rendering $\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X}$ \textbf{singular (non-invertible, determinant $= 0$)}. Standard regression solvers crash or produce numerically explosive, uninterpretable coefficient weights.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{5. Deterministic Parabolic Dependency ($Y = X^2$ on $X \in \{-3, -1, 0, 1, 3\}$)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Covariance Calculation:} $\bar{x} = 0$. $\sum x_i y_i = (-3)(9) + (-1)(1) + (0)(0) + (1)(1) + (3)(9) = -27 - 1 + 0 + 1 + 27 = 0$.
    $$s_{xy} = \frac{1}{4}(0 - 0) = \mathbf{0.0}$$
    \item \textbf{Pearson's $r$:} Since $s_{xy} = 0$, $r = \mathbf{0.0}$.
    \item \textbf{Independence Failure:} $Y$ is $100\%$ deterministically determined by $X$ ($Y = X^2$). Knowing $X$ completely removes all uncertainty in $Y$. Pearson's $r = 0$ indicates only the absence of a \emph{linear} slope, not statistical independence.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{6. Conceptual Diagnostic: Invariance Under Coordinate Transformations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (b)}
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is correct:} Under linear transformations $U = aX + b$ and $V = cY + d$, the Pearson correlation satisfies $r_{uv} = \operatorname{sign}(a \cdot c) \cdot r_{xy}$. Here, $a = -3$ (negative) and $c = +2$ (positive). The product $a \cdot c = (-3)(2) = -6 < 0$. Therefore, the sign of the covariance is inverted while the correlation magnitude remains invariant:
    $$r_{uv} = (-1) \cdot (+0.72) = \mathbf{-0.72}$$
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is false:} Linear invariance applies without sign change only when both scale factors have the same sign ($a \cdot c > 0$).
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} Correlation is normalized by standard deviations and cannot exceed the $[-1, +1]$ bound.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} Shift constants $+15$ and $-50$ cancel out in $(u_i - \bar{u})$ and have zero effect on correlation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{7. Conceptual Diagnostic: Anscombe's Quartet and Exploratory Verification}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (b)}
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is correct:} Francis Anscombe proved that summary statistics (mean, variance, and correlation $r = 0.816$) are identical across four fundamentally different datasets, including a perfect parabolic relationship (Dataset 2) and a vertical cluster with a single high-leverage outlier (Dataset 4). Relying on numerical correlation without graphical verification can mistake severe non-linearities or single data corruption points for valid linear relationships.
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is false:} By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, $|r| \le 1$ always, regardless of residual distribution.
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} Pearson's $r$ is specifically defined for numerical (interval/ratio) variables.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} Pearson's $r$ is a parametric linear statistic, not non-parametric.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Week 5: Principles of Counting, Permutations \& Combinations}

\subsection*{Detailed Solutions}

\textbf{1. Cascading Rule of Product}
A multi-stage deployment decision:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Authentication: $n_1 = 3$ mutually exclusive options.

    $$\P(K \mid R) = \frac{\P(K \cap R)}{\P(R)} = \frac{2/52}{26/52} = \frac{2}{26} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{13} \approx 0.0769}$$
    \item \textbf{Given Face Card ($F$):} There are 4 Kings out of 12 Face cards:
    $$\P(K \mid F) = \frac{\P(K \cap F)}{\P(F)} = \frac{4/52}{12/52} = \frac{4}{12} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3} \approx 0.3333}$$
    \item \textbf{Independence Check for $K$ and $R$:}

\section*{Week 12: Continuous Probability Distributions}

\textbf{1. Validating a Continuous PDF}
Given $f_X(x) = c \cdot x^2$ for $0 \le x \le 3$, and $0$ otherwise:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Normalization Constant $c$:}
    The total area under the PDF must equal $1.00$:
    $$\int_0^3 c x^2 \, dx = 1 \implies c \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^3 = c \left( \frac{27}{3} \right) = 9c = 1 \implies \mathbf{c = \frac{1}{9}}$$
    Thus, $f_X(x) = \frac{x^2}{9}$ for $x \in [0, 3]$.
    \item \textbf{Cumulative Distribution Function $F_X(x)$:}
    For $x < 0$, $F_X(x) = 0$. For $0 \le x \le 3$:
    $$F_X(x) = \int_0^x \frac{t^2}{9} \, dt = \left[ \frac{t^3}{27} \right]_0^x = \mathbf{\frac{x^3}{27}}$$
    For $x > 3$, $F_X(x) = 1.00$. Summarizing:
    $$F_X(x) = \begin{cases}
    0, & x < 0 \\
    \frac{x^3}{27}, & 0 \le x \le 3 \\
    1, & x > 3
    \end{cases}$$
    \item \textbf{Probability $\P(1 \le X \le 2)$:}
    $$\P(1 \le X \le 2) = F_X(2) - F_X(1) = \frac{2^3}{27} - \frac{1^3}{27} = \frac{8 - 1}{27} = \mathbf{\frac{7}{27} \approx 0.2593 \quad (25.93\%)}$$
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{2. Uniform Waiting Time at Rapid Transit ($X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 8)$)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mathematical PDF:}
    $$f_X(x) = \begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{8 - 0} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{8} = 0.125}, & 0 \le x \le 8 \\
    0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}$$
    The height is constant at $0.125\,\text{min}^{-1}$.
    \item \textbf{Mean and Variance:}
    $$\E[X] = \frac{a + b}{2} = \frac{0 + 8}{2} = \mathbf{4.0 \text{ minutes}}$$
    $$\Var(X) = \frac{(b - a)^2}{12} = \frac{(8 - 0)^2}{12} = \frac{64}{12} = \mathbf{\frac{16}{3} \approx 5.3333 \text{ min}^2}$$
    \item \textbf{Probability $\P(3 < X < 7)$:}
    Since $X$ is continuous, boundary strictness does not affect the probability:
    $$\P(3 < X < 7) = \int_3^7 \frac{1}{8} \, dx = \frac{7 - 3}{8} = \frac{4}{8} = \mathbf{0.50 \quad (50.0\%)}$$
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{3. Exponential Cloud Server Latency ($T \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda = 0.4)$)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expected Latency and Standard Deviation:}
    $$\E[T] = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{0.4} = \mathbf{2.5 \text{ seconds}}$$
    $$\SD(T) = \sqrt{\Var(T)} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda^2}} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \mathbf{2.5 \text{ seconds}}$$

    $$\P(T \le 2.5) = 1 - e^{-0.4 \times 2.5} = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 1 - 0.367879 = \mathbf{0.6321 \quad (63.21\%)}$$
    (Note: By definition, the probability of completing before the mean $\E[T]$ in any Exponential distribution is always $1 - e^{-1} \approx 63.2\%$).

    Using the survival function $\P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}$:
    $$\P(T > 5) = e^{-0.4 \times 5} = e^{-2} \approx \mathbf{0.1353 \quad (13.53\%)}$$

\textbf{4. Memoryless Property in Technical Support ($\lambda = 0.1$)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Conditional Probability $\P(X \ge 25 \mid X \ge 15)$:}
    By the Memoryless Property of the Exponential distribution:
    $$\P(X \ge s + t \mid X \ge s) = \P(X \ge t)$$
    Setting $s = 15$ and $t = 10$ ($s + t = 25$):
    $$\P(X \ge 25 \mid X \ge 15) = \P(X \ge 10) = e^{-\lambda (10)} = e^{-0.1 \times 10} = e^{-1} \approx \mathbf{0.3679 \quad (36.79\%)}$$
    \item \textbf{Comparison to Unconditional Probability:}
    A fresh call lasting at least 10 minutes has probability:
    $$\P(X \ge 10) = e^{-0.1 \times 10} = e^{-1} \approx \mathbf{0.3679}$$
    Both probabilities are \textbf{identically equal}. The elapsed 15 minutes of call duration provides zero information regarding remaining time; the distribution "forgets" the past.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{5. Data Science Modeling Architecture: Counts vs. Intervals}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Variable 1 (Transactions completed in the last hour):} Must be modeled using the \textbf{Poisson Distribution} ($\text{Poisson}(\lambda)$). This is a discrete random variable measuring integer event arrivals ($k \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$) across a fixed duration.
    \item \textbf{Variable 2 (Continuous duration between consecutive transactions):} Must be modeled using the \textbf{Exponential Distribution} ($\text{Exp}(\lambda)$). This is a continuous non-negative variable measuring elapsed time ($t \in [0, \infty)$) between Poisson arrivals.
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Duality:} In any Poisson process with constant rate $\lambda$, the count of arrivals in time interval $t$ follows $\text{Poisson}(\lambda t)$, while the inter-arrival durations between successive events are independent and identically distributed (IID) as $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{6. Conceptual Diagnostic: Probability Density Function Value Interpretation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (b)}
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is correct:} $f_X(x)$ is a probability \emph{density} function (units: probability per unit $x$), not a discrete probability mass. Only discrete PMFs are strictly bounded by $p_X(x) \le 1.0$. For continuous PDFs, $f_X(x) \ge 0$ is required, and $f_X(x)$ can exceed $1.0$ arbitrarily whenever the random variable is concentrated over an interval of width less than $1.0$. The only normalization axiom is that the total integral $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f_X(x) \, dx = 1.00$, which holds perfectly: $\int_0^{0.25} 4.0 \, dx = 4.0 \times 0.25 = 1.00$.
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is false:} The height of a PDF can be arbitrarily large (e.g., $f_X(x) = 1{,}000$ on $[0, 0.001]$).
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} $f_X(x)$ is the PDF, not the CDF (the CDF is $F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(t) dt$, which is strictly bounded in $[0, 1]$).
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} Continuous variables can be defined on intervals of any positive width, no matter how small.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{7. Conceptual Diagnostic: Memoryless Reliability Fallacy}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct Answer: (b)}
    \item \textbf{Why (b) is correct:} The Exponential distribution possesses the unique memoryless property: $\P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = \P(X > t)$. In physical terms, this asserts that a component that has already operated for 500 hours is as reliable as a brand-new component with 0 hours of wear. For physical mechanical assets (such as drone rotors, jet engines, and bearings) subject to friction, material fatigue, and wear-and-tear, hazard rates increase over time ($h(t)$ is monotonically increasing). Modeling such systems with an Exponential distribution severely underestimates failure rates at older ages. The standard mathematical model for aging systems is the \textbf{Weibull Distribution} with shape parameter $k > 1$.
    \item \textbf{Why (a) is false:} Infinite support is standard for survival distributions and is mathematically handled by tail decay.
    \item \textbf{Why (c) is false:} The Exponential distribution is continuous, matching flight hours.
    \item \textbf{Why (d) is false:} The mean and variance relationship ($\Var = 1/\lambda^2 = (\E[X])^2$) does not violate mechanical physics; the failure is the constant hazard rate assumption.
\end{itemize}
