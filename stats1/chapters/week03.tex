\chapter{Describing Numerical Data: Central Tendency}

Numerical data requires a richer mathematical toolkit than categorical data. Because numerical values occupy an interval or ratio scale, we can execute arithmetic operations to quantify the geometry of a distribution. When exploring a continuous or discrete numerical feature, statistical inquiry begins with three questions: Where is the distribution centred? How dispersed are the values? And what is the shape of its skewness? In this chapter, we master \textbf{Class Intervals and Histograms}, the \textbf{Arithmetic Mean}, \textbf{Weighted Mean}, \textbf{Median}, \textbf{Mode}, \textbf{Trimmed Mean}, their fundamental \textbf{Optimization Properties ($L_1$ vs. $L_2$ loss)}, and the cognitive traps of skewness and non-resistant averages.

\section{Frequency Tables and Histograms}

For continuous numerical data (which can assume uncountably infinite values), we cannot tabulate individual frequencies. Instead, the continuous support is partitioned into $k$ contiguous, non-overlapping \index{Class Intervals}\textbf{class intervals} (bins) $[b_0, b_1), [b_1, b_2), \dots, [b_{k-1}, b_k)$.

\begin{definitionbox}{Class Intervals and Histograms}
\index{Histogram}
Let $n$ observations span the range $R = x_{(n)} - x_{(1)}$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Equal Bin Width ($w$):} Given $k$ bins:
    $$w = \frac{\max(x) - \min(x)}{k}$$
    Standard heuristics for choosing $k$ include \textbf{Sturges' Rule} ($k = 1 + \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$) and \textbf{Freedman-Diaconis Rule} ($w = 2 \cdot \text{IQR} \cdot n^{-1/3}$).
    \item \textbf{Histogram Structure:} A plot of contiguous vertical columns where each bar's base represents a class interval on the continuous number line, and its area (or height) represents frequency or relative frequency.
    \item \textbf{Adjacent Bars Rule:} Unlike categorical bar charts, histogram bars \textbf{touch without gaps} to reflect the underlying continuity of the real number line. Gaps appear only if a bin contains exactly zero observed data points.
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\section{Measures of Central Tendency}

A measure of central tendency provides a single summary value representing the location or center of a numerical distribution.

\begin{definitionbox}{The Arithmetic Mean and Weighted Mean}
\index{Arithmetic Mean}\index{Weighted Mean}
Let $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ be a sample of $n$ numerical observations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sample Arithmetic Mean ($\bar{x}$):}
    $$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$
    \item \textbf{Weighted Arithmetic Mean ($\bar{x}_w$):} When each observation $x_i$ carries an importance weight or frequency count $w_i > 0$:
    $$\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i}$$
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{theorembox}{The Zero-Sum Property of Deviations}
\index{Zero-Sum Property}
The sum of algebraic signed deviations of all observations from their arithmetic mean is \textbf{identically zero}:
$$\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i - \sum_{i=1}^n \bar{x} = n\bar{x} - n\bar{x} = 0$$
The arithmetic mean represents the exact physical "center of mass" (fulcrum) where positive and negative deviations perfectly balance.
\end{theorembox}

\begin{definitionbox}{The Sample Median and Mode}
\index{Median}\index{Mode}
Let $x_{(1)} \le x_{(2)} \le \dots \le x_{(n)}$ denote the order statistics (sorted sample).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sample Median ($\tilde{x}$ or $Q_2$):}
    $$\tilde{x} = \begin{cases}
    x_{\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}, & \text{if } n \text{ is odd} \\[8pt]
    \frac{x_{\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)} + x_{\left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right)}}{2}, & \text{if } n \text{ is even}
    \end{cases}$$
    \item \textbf{Mode:} The value that occurs with the greatest frequency. For continuous distributions, the mode is the point $x$ where the probability density function $f(x)$ attains its global maximum.
    \item \textbf{Trimmed Mean ($\bar{x}_{\alpha}$):} To balance resistance with statistical efficiency, the $\alpha$-trimmed mean discards the lowest $\alpha\%$ and highest $\alpha\%$ of observations before computing the arithmetic mean:
    $$\bar{x}_{\alpha} = \frac{1}{n - 2k} \sum_{i=k+1}^{n-k} x_{(i)} \quad \text{where } k = \lfloor n \alpha \rfloor$$
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\section{The Optimization Duality: $L_1$ vs. $L_2$ Loss}

Why do both the Mean and Median exist? In mathematical statistics and machine learning, they arise as optimal solutions to two distinct loss minimization problems:

\begin{theorembox}{Loss Minimization Properties of Mean and Median}
\index{Loss Minimization}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Mean Minimizes $L_2$ Squared Error:} The constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$ that minimizes the sum of squared distances from the data points is the \textbf{Arithmetic Mean}:
    $$\arg\min_{c \in \mathbb{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - c)^2 = \bar{x}$$
    \textit{Proof:} Differentiating $g(c) = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - c)^2$ with respect to $c$:
    $$g'(c) = -2\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - c) = 0 \implies \sum_{i=1}^n x_i - n c = 0 \implies c = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \bar{x}$$
    \item \textbf{The Median Minimizes $L_1$ Absolute Error:} The constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$ that minimizes the sum of absolute distances from the data points is the \textbf{Sample Median}:
    $$\arg\min_{c \in \mathbb{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i - c| = \tilde{x}$$
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Data Science Impact:} Mean squared error (MSE) regression estimates the conditional mean $\E[Y \mid X]$. Mean absolute error (MAE) regression estimates the conditional median $\text{Median}(Y \mid X)$, providing robust predictions immune to outlier anomalies!
\end{theorembox}

\section{Cognitive Traps in Central Tendency}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 1: The Non-Resistance of the Arithmetic Mean}
The arithmetic mean has a \textbf{breakdown point of 0\%}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A single corrupted or extreme observation $x_k \to \infty$ pulls the sample mean $\bar{x} \to \infty$, regardless of how large the sample size $n$ is.
    \item In income distributions, housing prices, or website response latencies, reporting the "average" gives a grossly distorted picture. Always verify skewness and report the \textbf{Median} alongside the Mean.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 2: The "Average of Averages" Fallacy}
Never compute the simple unweighted arithmetic mean of group averages unless all groups have \textbf{strictly identical sample sizes}!
\begin{itemize}
    \item Suppose Section 1 has $n_1 = 10$ students with average exam score $\bar{x}_1 = 90$. Section 2 has $n_2 = 90$ students with average score $\bar{x}_2 = 50$.
    \item Naive Average: $\frac{90 + 50}{2} = 70$. (Completely wrong!)
    \item Correct Weighted Mean:
    $$\bar{x}_{\text{combined}} = \frac{n_1 \bar{x}_1 + n_2 \bar{x}_2}{n_1 + n_2} = \frac{10(90) + 90(50)}{10 + 90} = \frac{900 + 4500}{100} = \mathbf{54.0}$$
    Ignoring group sizes gives $9 \times$ too much weight to the small section!
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\section{Python Implementation: Measures of Central Tendency in SciPy}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Central Tendency and Loss Minimization in Python}]
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

# Right-skewed distribution (e.g. software engineer salaries)
salaries = np.array([60, 65, 70, 72, 75, 80, 85, 90, 110, 1500])  # in thousands

mean_val = np.mean(salaries)
median_val = np.median(salaries)
mode_val = stats.mode(salaries, keepdims=True).mode[0]
trim_mean = stats.trim_mean(salaries, proportiontocut=0.10) # 10% trimmed mean

print(f"Mean:   ${mean_val:.1f}k  (Distorted by the single $1500k executive)")
print(f"Median: ${median_val:.1f}k  (Robust summary of typical salary)")
print(f"Mode:   ${mode_val:.1f}k")
print(f"10% Trimmed Mean: ${trim_mean:.1f}k")

# Numerical proof of optimization duality
c_grid = np.linspace(50, 150, 1000)
l2_losses = [np.sum((salaries - c)**2) for c in c_grid]
l1_losses = [np.sum(np.abs(salaries - c)) for c in c_grid]

print(f"\nEmpirical argmin of L2 loss: {c_grid[np.argmin(l2_losses)]:.2f} (Matches Mean)")
print(f"Empirical argmin of L1 loss: {c_grid[np.argmin(l1_losses)]:.2f} (Matches Median)")
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Part A: Computational and Analytical Problems}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Outlier Sensitivity and Skewness:} A software development team consists of 6 junior engineers and 1 director. The monthly compensations (in INR) are:
    $$\{70{,}000,\; 75{,}000,\; 80{,}000,\; 80{,}000,\; 85{,}000,\; 90{,}000,\; 1{,}480{,}000\}$$
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compute the Sample Mean ($\bar{x}$) and Sample Median ($\tilde{x}$).
        \item Compute the $14.3\%$ trimmed mean (dropping the single minimum and single maximum).
        \item Characterize the direction of skewness based on the relationship between $\bar{x}$ and $\tilde{x}$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{The Zero-Sum Deviation Property:} For the dataset $X = \{12, 15, 18, 22, 28\}$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compute the sample mean $\bar{x}$.
        \item Calculate each deviation $(x_i - \bar{x})$ and algebraically verify that $\sum_{i=1}^5 (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Weighted Mean of Aggregated Batches:} An automated quality inspector monitors component thickness across three manufacturing shifts:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Shift A: $n_1 = 500$ parts produced, sample mean thickness $\bar{x}_1 = 12.4\,\text{mm}$.
        \item Shift B: $n_2 = 300$ parts produced, sample mean thickness $\bar{x}_2 = 12.8\,\text{mm}$.
        \item Shift C: $n_3 = 200$ parts produced, sample mean thickness $\bar{x}_3 = 13.5\,\text{mm}$.
    \end{itemize}
    Compute the grand mean thickness $\bar{x}_{\text{combined}}$ across the entire day's production of 1,000 parts.

    \item \textbf{Optimization Property Verification:} Given three data points $\{2, 5, 11\}$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Find the value of $c$ that minimizes the sum of squared errors $f(c) = \sum_{i=1}^3 (x_i - c)^2$.
        \item Find the value of $c$ that minimizes the sum of absolute errors $g(c) = \sum_{i=1}^3 |x_i - c|$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Histogram Binning Impact:} A dataset of $n = 100$ response times spans from $12\,\text{ms}$ to $92\,\text{ms}$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Using the square-root rule $k \approx \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$, determine the recommended number of class intervals.
        \item Calculate the required equal bin width $w$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Part B: Conceptual and Diagnostic Multiple Choice Questions}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: Loss Functions and Model Invariance} \\
A financial risk modeling team is training a loss forecasting model for credit default events. The loss distribution exhibits extreme right-skewness, with rare defaults producing multi-million-dollar catastrophic losses. The executive board requires a model that outputs a point forecast $\hat{y}$ that is \textbf{robust to extreme outlier defaults} and minimizes the expected absolute monetary deviation $\E[|Y - \hat{y}|]$. Which objective function and summary statistic should the engineering team deploy?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Train the model with Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, because MSE minimizes the arithmetic mean $\bar{y}$.
    \item Train the model with Mean Absolute Error (MAE) loss, because the point that minimizes sum of absolute deviations is the conditional median, which is immune to the magnitude of extreme right-tail outliers.
    \item Train the model with Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), because MAPE is strictly invariant to zero values.
    \item Train the model using the Mode, because the mode minimizes both $L_1$ and $L_2$ losses simultaneously in continuous distributions.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: Aggregation of Group Averages} \\
A national tech conglomerate operates three subsidiaries: Alpha ($100$ employees, average salary $\$120{,}000$), Beta ($300$ employees, average salary $\$80{,}000$), and Gamma ($600$ employees, average salary $\$50{,}000$). The Chief People Officer calculates:
$$\text{Average} = \frac{\$120{,}000 + \$80{,}000 + \$50{,}000}{3} = \frac{\$250{,}000}{3} \approx \$83{,}333$$
and announces that the average salary in the conglomerate is over $\$83{,}000$. What is the true corporate mean salary, and why did the officer's calculation fail?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The true mean is $\$66{,}000$; the officer's calculation failed by assigning equal $1/3$ weight to all three subsidiaries instead of weighting each subsidiary proportional to its employee headcount.
    \item The true mean is $\$83{,}333$; the officer's formula is exact because all three companies are part of the same parent corporation.
    \item The true mean is $\$50{,}000$; because Gamma has the largest headcount ($600$), its mean overrides all other subsidiaries.
    \item The true mean is $\$70{,}000$; because the median of $120{,}000, 80{,}000, 50{,}000$ must replace the arithmetic average.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
