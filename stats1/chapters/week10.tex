\chapter{The Bernoulli and Binomial Distributions}

In previous chapters, we analyzed generic, custom-constructed discrete random variables. However, applied data science and statistical computing frequently encounter repeating, canonical structures. Rather than deriving probability laws from scratch for every business experiment, mathematicians rely on standard parametric families. The most foundational parametric discrete family in statistics, digital experimentation (A/B testing), and classification is the \textbf{Bernoulli process} and the resulting \textbf{Binomial Distribution}. \section{The Bernoulli Distribution}

The elementary building block of binary statistical modeling is the single two-outcome trial.

\begin{definitionbox}{Bernoulli Trial and Distribution}
\index{Bernoulli Distribution}
A \textbf{Bernoulli Trial} is a random experiment possessing exactly two mutually exclusive outcomes: \emph{"Success"} (conventionally encoded as $1$) and \emph{"Failure"} (encoded as $0$).
Let the success parameter be $p = \P(X = 1) \in [0, 1]$, and failure probability be $q = 1 - p = \P(X = 0)$.
The random variable $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ has PMF:
$$p_X(x) = \begin{cases}
p^x (1 - p)^{1 - x}, & x \in \{0, 1\} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$$
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expected Value:} $\E[X] = 1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot (1 - p) = \mathbf{p}$.
    \item \textbf{Second Moment:} $\E[X^2] = 1^2 \cdot p + 0^2 \cdot (1 - p) = \mathbf{p}$.
    \item \textbf{Theoretical Variance:} $\Var(X) = \E[X^2] - (\E[X])^2 = p - p^2 = \mathbf{p(1 - p)}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\section{Sequences of IID Bernoulli Trials}

\begin{definitionbox}{The IID Assumption (Independent and Identically Distributed)}
\index{IID Assumption}
A sequence of binary random variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ constitutes an \textbf{IID Bernoulli sequence} if and only if:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Identical Distribution (Stationarity):} Every individual trial shares the exact same success probability:
    $$\P(X_i = 1) = p \quad \text{for all } i = 1, 2, \dots, n$$
    \item \textbf{Mutual Independence:} The outcome of trial $i$ carries zero statistical influence over trial $j$ for any $i \neq j$:
    $$\P(X_1 = x_1, X_2 = x_2, \dots, X_n = x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n \P(X_i = x_i)$$
\end{enumerate}
\end{definitionbox}

\section{The Binomial Distribution}

When we aggregate $n$ independent and identically distributed Bernoulli trials, the total count of observed successes defines the Binomial distribution.

\begin{definitionbox}{The Binomial Distribution}
\index{Binomial Distribution}
Let $X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ represent the total number of successes in $n$ IID $\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ trials. We write:
$$X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$$
with support $\mathcal{R}_X = \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\}$.
The Probability Mass Function (PMF) is given by:
$$p_X(k) = \P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k} \quad \text{for } k \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$$
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}{Deriving the Binomial PMF: Deconstructing the Terms}
Why does the Binomial PMF take this exact mathematical form? It is the product of three distinct components:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Probability of $k$ Successes:} Each success occurs with probability $p$. For $k$ independent successes, the joint probability is $p^k$.
    \item \textbf{Probability of $(n-k)$ Failures:} The remaining $n - k$ trials must be failures, contributing $(1-p)^{n-k}$.
    \item \textbf{The Combinations Multiplier $\binom{n}{k}$:} The quantity $p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$ represents the probability of \emph{one specific sequence} of outcomes (e.g. $S S \dots S F F \dots F$). But the $k$ successes can occur in any arbitrary positions across the $n$ trial slots! The number of distinct ordered sequences containing exactly $k$ successes is $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$.
\end{enumerate}
Summing across all mutually exclusive sequences yields $\binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$!
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorembox}{Expectation and Variance of the Binomial Distribution}
\index{Expectation of Binomial}\index{Variance of Binomial}
Rather than evaluating tedious combinatorial summations, we leverage the indicator representation $X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$, where each $X_i \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(p)$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expected Value (Linearity):}
    $$\E[X] = \E\left[\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right] = \sum_{i=1}^n \E[X_i] = \sum_{i=1}^n p = \mathbf{np}$$
    \item \textbf{Variance (Independence):} Because the trials are mutually independent:
    $$\Var(X) = \Var\left[\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right] = \sum_{i=1}^n \Var(X_i) = \sum_{i=1}^n p(1 - p) = \mathbf{np(1 - p)}$$
    \item \textbf{Standard Deviation:} $\sigma_X = \sqrt{np(1 - p)}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorembox}

\section{Cognitive Traps in Binomial Modeling}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 1: The Omitted Combinations Term $\binom{n}{k}$}
A common student mistake is writing $\P(X = k) = p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item In tossing 3 coins, the probability of obtaining 2 heads is \textbf{not} $(0.5)^2 (0.5)^1 = 0.125$.
    \item $0.125$ is the probability of the single ordered sequence $HHT$.
    \item Two heads can occur as $HHT$, $HTH$, or $THH$. Multiplying by $\binom{3}{2} = 3$ gives the true probability $3 \times 0.125 = \mathbf{0.375}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 2: Sampling Without Replacement (Hypergeometric Breakdown)}
\index{Hypergeometric Distribution}
The Binomial distribution fundamentally mandates that $p$ remains \textbf{strictly constant} across all trials.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Suppose an urn contains $N = 10$ marbles (3 red, 7 blue). If you sample $n = 4$ marbles \textbf{without replacement}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The probability of the first marble being red is $3/10 = 0.30$.
        \item If the first marble was red, the probability of the second marble being red drops to $2/9 \approx 0.222$.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Because trials are dependent, the Binomial distribution fails! The exact distribution is the \textbf{Hypergeometric Distribution}:
    $$\P(X = k) = \frac{\binom{K}{k}\binom{N - K}{n - k}}{\binom{N}{n}}$$
    \item \textbf{Rule of Thumb:} The Binomial distribution is an acceptable approximation for sampling without replacement \textbf{only if} the sample size is small relative to the population: $n \le 0.05 N$ (the 5\% rule).
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 3: Maximum Variance of a Binary Process}
The variance of a Binomial distribution $g(p) = np(1-p)$ is a quadratic parabola.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Differentiating $g'(p) = n(1 - 2p) = 0 \implies p = 0.50$.
    \item Maximum uncertainty occurs when outcomes are maximally unpredictable ($p = 0.50$), yielding $\Var(X)_{\max} = 0.25n$.
    \item As $p \to 0$ or $p \to 1$, the outcome becomes deterministic, and variance vanishes: $\Var(X) \to 0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\section{Python Implementation: Binomial PMF, CDF, and Simulation}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Binomial Probability Calculations and Convergence in Python}]
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binom

# Parameters: A/B testing conversion tracking (n=100 users, p=0.08 conversion rate)
n, p = 100, 0.08

# 1. Exact PMF probability of observing exactly 8 conversions
p_exact_8 = binom.pmf(k=8, n=n, p=p)
print(f"P(X = 8)  = {p_exact_8:.4f}")

# 2. Cumulative probability of at most 8 conversions (CDF)
p_at_most_8 = binom.cdf(k=8, n=n, p=p)
print(f"P(X <= 8) = {p_at_most_8:.4f}")

# 3. Probability of at least 10 conversions: P(X >= 10) = 1 - P(X <= 9)
p_at_least_10 = 1.0 - binom.cdf(k=9, n=n, p=p)
print(f"P(X >= 10)= {p_at_least_10:.4f}")

# 4. Moments verification
mean_theoretical = n * p
var_theoretical = n * p * (1.0 - p)
print(f"\nTheoretical Mean     = {mean_theoretical:.2f}")
print(f"Theoretical Variance = {var_theoretical:.2f}")
print(f"Theoretical Std Dev  = {np.sqrt(var_theoretical):.2f}")
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Part A: Computational and Analytical Problems}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single Bernoulli User Engagement:} An algorithmic recommender system delivers an ad impression. The click-through probability is $p = 0.04$. Let $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.04)$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item State the PMF of $X$.
        \item Compute the expected value $\E[X]$ and variance $\Var(X)$.
        \item Compute the standard deviation $\SD(X)$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Exact Binomial Calculations on Assembly Lines:} An industrial robotics workstation produces printed circuit boards with a known defect rate of $p = 0.05$. An inspector draws an IID sample of $n = 6$ boards from the line.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compute the probability that exactly 0 boards are defective.
        \item Compute the probability that exactly 2 boards are defective.
        \item Compute the probability that at least 1 board is defective using the complement rule.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Standardized Guessing Exam Score:} A standardized multiple choice test contains $n = 60$ questions, each featuring 4 possible options with exactly 1 correct answer ($p = 0.25$). A candidate answers every question by pure random guessing.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item State the expected score $\E[Y]$ and standard deviation $\SD(Y)$.
        \item What is the candidate's $z$-score if they miraculously score $Y = 30$?
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Independent Replicated Microservices:} A payment gateway routes requests across $n = 5$ independent parallel processing worker instances. Each instance has an independent failure probability of $q = 0.10$ ($p = 0.90$ operational). A payment succeeds if \textbf{at least 3 worker instances} respond successfully.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item State the formal Binomial summation for the success probability: $\P(X \ge 3)$.
        \item Compute the exact numerical probability that the payment gateway transaction succeeds.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Symmetry and Skewness of Binomial Distributions:} Consider two Binomial variables: $X_1 \sim \text{Binomial}(20, 0.50)$ and $X_2 \sim \text{Binomial}(20, 0.10)$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compare the skewness direction of $X_1$ versus $X_2$.
        \item Compute and compare the numerical variance of $X_1$ versus $X_2$. Which distribution has greater intrinsic uncertainty?
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Part B: Conceptual and Diagnostic Multiple Choice Questions}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: Sampling Without Replacement and Hypergeometric Breakdown} \\
A quality control technician inspects a small batch of $N = 20$ high-precision aerospace valves containing exactly $K = 4$ defective valves. The technician draws $n = 5$ valves sequentially \textbf{without replacement} and models the number of defective valves in the sample using $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n=5, p=0.20)$. Why is the technician's Binomial model mathematically invalid?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Because $n = 5$ is too large for the Binomial distribution.
    \item Because sampling without replacement from a small population violates the independence and constant success probability assumptions; the true distribution is Hypergeometric.
    \item Because the success probability $p = 0.20$ is too small, requiring a Poisson distribution.
    \item Because the Binomial distribution is restricted exclusively to continuous measurements.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: Maximum Variance of a Binary Process} \\
A product manager at an e-commerce platform wants to design an A/B test on checkout conversion rate $p$. The team tests various user interfaces with estimated conversion rates ranging between $1\%$ and $99\%$. For a fixed sample size of $n = 1{,}000$ users, at which conversion rate $p$ is the sampling variance $\Var(X)$ of the total number of converted users at its \textbf{global maximum}?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item At $p = 0.01$, because rare events produce volatile sample spikes.
    \item At $p = 0.50$, because the quadratic function $p(1 - p)$ achieves its unique mathematical maximum at $p = 0.50$, creating maximal Bernoulli uncertainty.
    \item At $p = 0.99$, because high success rates accumulate the largest sum of outcomes.
    \item The variance is constant across all $p \in (0, 1)$ because $n = 1{,}000$ is fixed.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
