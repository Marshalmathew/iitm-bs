\chapter{Algebra of Polynomials and Model Capacity}
\index{Algebra of Polynomials}

A polynomial is a mathematical expression consisting of variables and coefficients involving addition, subtraction, multiplication, and non-negative integer powers. In data science, machine learning, and numerical analysis, polynomials serve as fundamental basis functions: polynomial regression fits non-linear trends, Taylor approximations linearize complex loss functions, and polynomial kernels empower Support Vector Machines to construct non-linear decision boundaries.

\section{Definitions and Classifications}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Data Science: Evaluating Polynomials]
import numpy as np

# Create a polynomial p(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 + x - 5
p = np.poly1d([1, -2, 1, -5])

# Evaluate at x=3
print(f'p(3) = {p(3)}') # Outputs 7
\end{lstlisting}


A real polynomial $P(x)$ of degree $n$ is defined as:
$$P(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$$
where $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n \in \R$ with $a_n \neq 0$, and $n \in \set{0, 1, 2, \dots}$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Degree ($n$):} The highest exponent of the variable $x$ with a non-zero coefficient.
    \item \textbf{Leading Coefficient ($a_n$):} The coefficient of the term with the highest degree.
    \item \textbf{Constant Term ($a_0$):} The term of degree $0$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{intuitionbox}{Polynomials as Lego Blocks}
Why do mathematicians care so much about $x^2, x^3$, and $x^4$? 
Think of these simple powers of $x$ as the "Lego blocks" of mathematics. By stacking them together and adjusting their coefficients ($a_i$), you can bend a curve to mimic almost any shape in the real world!
\begin{itemize}
    \item Need a straight ramp? Use $a_1 x$.
    \item Need a parabolic satellite dish? Add $a_2 x^2$.
    \item Need an S-curve that models population growth? Add $a_3 x^3$.
\end{itemize}
In calculus, you will later learn the \textbf{Taylor Series}, which proves that \textit{any} smooth, complicated function (like sine waves or logarithms) can be approximated perfectly just by stacking enough polynomial Lego blocks!
\end{intuitionbox}

\textbf{Data Science Relevance:} In \textbf{Polynomial Regression}, if a straight line (degree 1) doesn't fit the data well, we might fit a quadratic (degree 2) or cubic (degree 3) polynomial. However, choosing too high of a degree leads to "overfitting"—the model perfectly learns the training data but fails to generalize to new data.

\section{Polynomial Division and Theorems}

Like integers, polynomials can be divided.

\begin{theorembox}{Division Algorithm for Polynomials}
For any dividend polynomial $P(x)$ and non-zero divisor polynomial $D(x)$, there exist unique polynomials $Q(x)$ (quotient) and $R(x)$ (remainder) such that:
$$P(x) = D(x)Q(x) + R(x)$$
where either $R(x) = 0$ or $\text{deg}(R) < \text{deg}(D)$.
\end{theorembox}

\begin{theorembox}{Remainder and Factor Theorems}
Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial of degree $n \ge 1$ and let $c \in \R$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Remainder Theorem:} If $P(x)$ is divided by the linear term $(x - c)$, the remainder is exactly the value $P(c)$.
    \item \textbf{Factor Theorem:} The linear term $(x - c)$ is a factor of $P(x)$ if and only if $P(c) = 0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorembox}

\begin{examplebox}{Remainder Theorem and Factoring}
Determine if $(x - 2)$ is a factor of $P(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 4x - 4$, and find the remainder when divided.
\begin{itemize}
    \item By the Remainder Theorem, the remainder $R$ is equal to $P(2)$:
    $$P(2) = (2)^3 - 3(2)^2 + 4(2) - 4 = 8 - 3(4) + 8 - 4 = 8 - 12 + 8 - 4 = 0$$
    \item Since $P(2) = 0$, the remainder is $0$.
    \item By the Factor Theorem, because the remainder is $0$, $(x - 2)$ \textbf{is a factor} of $P(x)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{examplebox}

\section{Graphing Polynomials}

To sketch a polynomial function $P(x) = a_n x^n + \dots + a_0$, we analyze three key features: End Behavior, Intercepts/Multiplicities, and Turning Points.

\subsection{End Behavior}

The long-term behavior of $P(x)$ as $x \to \pm\infty$ is determined solely by its leading term $a_n x^n$.

$x \to -\infty, y \to \infty$ \\

\subsection{Intercepts and Root Multiplicities}

If $P(c) = 0$, then $c$ is a real zero (root) of $P(x)$ and $(c, 0)$ is an x-intercept.
If a factor $(x - c)$ appears repeated $k$ times (i.e., $(x - c)^k$ divides $P(x)$), then $c$ is a zero of \textbf{multiplicity $k$}.

\begin{definitionbox}{Multiplicity Behavior at X-intercepts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Odd Multiplicity ($k$ is odd):} The graph \textbf{crosses} the x-axis at $x = c$. If $k > 1$, the graph also flattens (inflection point) as it crosses.
    \item \textbf{Even Multiplicity ($k$ is even):} The graph is \textbf{tangent} to the x-axis (touches and bounces/turns back) at $x = c$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\subsection{Turning Points}

A turning point is a point where the graph changes direction from increasing to decreasing, or vice versa (local extrema).
\begin{theorembox}{Turning Points Bound}
A polynomial of degree $n$ can have \textbf{at most} $n - 1$ turning points.
\end{theorembox}

\begin{examplebox}{Polynomial Sketching Analysis}
Analyze the polynomial $f(x) = -(x - 1)^2(x + 2)$ for sketching.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Degree and Leading Coefficient:}
    Expanding the leading term: $f(x) = -(x^2)(x) + \dots = -x^3 + \dots$.
    The degree is $n = 3$ (odd), and the leading coefficient is $a_n = -1$ (negative).
    End behavior: As $x \to \infty, y \to -\infty$. As $x \to -\infty, y \to \infty$ (Up-Down).
    \item \textbf{Intercepts:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item y-intercept: $f(0) = -(0-1)^2(0+2) = -1(1)(2) = -2 \implies (0, -2)$.
        \item x-intercepts: Set $f(x) = 0 \implies (x-1)^2(x+2) = 0$.
        Zeros: $x = 1$ (multiplicity 2) and $x = -2$ (multiplicity 1).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Behavior at Zeros:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item At $x = 1$ (even multiplicity): The graph touches and bounces off the x-axis.
        \item At $x = -2$ (odd multiplicity): The graph crosses the x-axis.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Turning Points:} At most $n - 1 = 2$ turning points.
\end{enumerate}
\end{examplebox}

\section{Common Pitfalls, Traps \& Misconceptions}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 1: Runge's Phenomenon and High-Degree Interpolation Overfitting}
A central temptation in Data Science is to fit an $(N - 1)$-degree polynomial to $N$ data points to achieve $100\%$ training accuracy ($\text{SSE} = 0$).
\begin{itemize}
    \item By polynomial interpolation, an $(N-1)$-degree polynomial can fit $N$ points perfectly.
    \item However, as degree $n \ge 5$ grows, polynomials develop severe boundary oscillations—known as \textbf{Runge's Phenomenon}.
    \item Between training points and near interval boundaries, the fitted curve oscillates wildly toward $\pm \infty$, resulting in catastrophic generalization errors on test data.
    \item \textbf{Data Science Rule:} Never increase polynomial degree without regularization (Ridge/Lasso) or cross-validation!
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 2: Turning Points: "At Most $n-1$" vs. "Exactly $n-1$"}
Students frequently confuse the theoretical upper bound with the exact count of turning points.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A degree-$n$ polynomial has \textbf{at most} $n - 1$ turning points. It does not have to achieve this bound.
    \item \textbf{Example:} $f(x) = x^3$ has degree $n = 3$. Its maximum possible turning points is $3 - 1 = 2$.
    \item However, its derivative is $f'(x) = 3x^2 \ge 0$. The function is strictly monotonically increasing and has \textbf{zero} turning points (the stationary point at $x = 0$ is an inflection point, not an extremum).
    \item The number of real turning points always has the same parity as $n - 1$ (decreasing in pairs due to complex conjugate critical points).
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 3: Remainder Theorem Sign Inversion}
When dividing $P(x)$ by $(x + c)$, the remainder is $P(-c)$, \textbf{not} $P(c)$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Remainder Theorem states that division by $(x - c)$ yields remainder $P(c)$.
    \item For the linear divisor $(x + 4)$, rewrite as $(x - (-4))$. The remainder is $P(-4)$.
    \item Forgetting to flip the sign is the single most common cause of lost marks on polynomial division exams.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 4: Multiplicity and Axis Behavior}
The behavior of a polynomial at an $x$-intercept $(r, 0)$ depends on whether its multiplicity $m$ is even or odd:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Even Multiplicity ($m = 2, 4, 6, \dots$):} The factor $(x - r)^m \ge 0$ never changes sign. The graph is \textbf{tangent} to the $x$-axis and \textbf{bounces back} without crossing.
    \item \textbf{Odd Multiplicity ($m = 1, 3, 5, \dots$):} The factor changes sign. The graph \textbf{crosses} the $x$-axis. If $m \ge 3$, the curve flattens into an inflection point as it passes through $(r, 0)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\section{Python Implementation: Polynomial Fitting, Roots, and Overfitting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Polynomial Algebra and Overfitting Diagnostics in Python}]
import numpy as np
import sympy as sp

# 1. Remainder and Factor Theorem Verification with SymPy
x = sp.symbols('x', real=True)
P = x**4 - 2*x**3 + 5*x - 7

# Remainder when divided by (x - 2) is P(2)
c_val = 2
remainder_eval = P.subs(x, c_val)
quotient, rem_poly = sp.div(P, x - c_val)

print(f"P(2) via Substitution:       {remainder_eval}")
print(f"Polynomial Division Remainder: {rem_poly}")
assert remainder_eval == rem_poly, "Remainder Theorem Verified!"

# 2. Finding All Roots and Multiplicities
Q = (x - 3)**2 * (x + 1)**3 * (x - 5)
roots_dict = sp.roots(Q, x)
print("\nRoots and Multiplicities of Q(x):")
for r, mult in roots_dict.items():
    behavior = "Touches/Bounces (Even)" if mult % 2 == 0 else "Crosses (Odd)"
    print(f"  Root x = {r:2d} | Multiplicity = {mult} -> Graph {behavior}")

# 3. Polynomial Overfitting (Runge's Phenomenon Simulation)
np.random.seed(42)
x_train = np.linspace(-1, 1, 6)
y_train = 1.0 / (1.0 + 25.0 * x_train**2)  # Runge's function

# Fit degree 1 (underfit) vs degree 5 (exact interpolation)
p1 = np.poly1d(np.polyfit(x_train, y_train, deg=1))
p5 = np.poly1d(np.polyfit(x_train, y_train, deg=5))

# Evaluate at dense test points
x_dense = np.linspace(-1, 1, 100)
y_true = 1.0 / (1.0 + 25.0 * x_dense**2)
y_pred_5 = p5(x_dense)

mse_train_5 = np.mean((p5(x_train) - y_train)**2)
mse_test_5 = np.mean((y_pred_5 - y_true)**2)
print(f"\nDegree 5 Interpolation:")
print(f"  Training MSE: {mse_train_5:.6f} (Near 0 -> Perfect memorization)")
print(f"  Dense Test MSE: {mse_test_5:.6f} (High error due to edge oscillations!)")
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Part B: Conceptual and Diagnostic Multiple Choice Questions}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: Turning Points Upper Bound Fallacy} \\
A student inspects the fifth-degree polynomial $P(x) = x^5 + 2x^3 + 4x - 7$ and states: \textit{"Because the degree is $n = 5$, the graph must have exactly $5 - 1 = 4$ turning points."} Which of the following provides the \textbf{correct mathematical refutation}?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The student is incorrect because turning points must always equal the degree $n = 5$.
    \item The student is incorrect because $n - 1$ is only an \textbf{upper bound} ($\le n - 1$); evaluating the derivative $P'(x) = 5x^4 + 6x^2 + 4 > 0$ reveals that $P'(x)$ has no real roots, so $P(x)$ has \textbf{zero} turning points.
    \item The student is incorrect because fifth-degree polynomials always have an even number of turning points.
    \item The student is correct because odd-degree polynomials always achieve their maximum theoretical turning points.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: Multiplicity and Sign Preservation} \\
Let $P(x) = (x - c)^k Q(x)$, where $Q(c) \neq 0$. If $k$ is an \textbf{even positive integer}, what is the geometric and algebraic behavior of $P(x)$ in an open neighborhood around $x = c$?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $P(x)$ changes sign as $x$ passes through $c$, crossing the $x$-axis perpendicularly.
    \item $P(x)$ has a jump discontinuity at $x = c$.
    \item $(x - c)^k \ge 0$ maintains the same sign on both sides of $c$; the graph is tangent to the $x$-axis and bounces back without crossing.
    \item The graph flattens into an inflection point and crosses the $x$-axis.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
