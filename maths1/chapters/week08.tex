\chapter{Differential Calculus and Optimization}

Differentiation formalizes the concept of instantaneous rate of change. In machine learning, deep neural networks, and statistical optimization, differential calculus is the algorithmic engine of learning: the chain rule powers backpropagation, the gradient points along the path of steepest ascent, and second derivatives (Hessians and curvature) dictate optimization stability and adaptive step sizes.

\section{Limits, Continuity, and Differentiability}

\begin{intuitionbox}{The Speedometer of Math}
Differentiation is the mathematical equivalent of looking at your car's speedometer. While algebra can only tell you your average speed over an hour, a derivative tells you your exact speed at a specific split-second in time. It measures the instantaneous rate at which any quantity is changing.
\end{intuitionbox}

Let $f(x)$ be a function defined on an open interval containing $c$.

\begin{definitionbox}{Limit, Continuity, and Derivative}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limit:} We say $\limit{x}{c} f(x) = L$ if we can make the values of $f(x)$ arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $c$. This requires the left-hand limit and right-hand limit to be equal:
    $$\limit{x}{c^-} f(x) = \limit{x}{c^+} f(x) = L$$
    \item \textbf{Continuity:} $f(x)$ is continuous at $x = c$ if the limit exists and equals the function's value:
    $$\limit{x}{c} f(x) = f(c)$$
    \item \index{Derivative}\textbf{Derivative:} The derivative of $f(x)$ at $x$, denoted $f'(x)$ or $\deriv{y}{x}$, is defined as the limit of the difference quotient:
    $$f'(x) = \limit{h}{0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$$
    If this limit exists, $f$ is \textbf{differentiable} at $x$. Differentiability strictly implies continuity, but continuity does \textbf{not} guarantee differentiability (e.g., $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $x=0$, but has a sharp corner so the derivative is undefined).
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\section{Rules of Differentiation}

Calculating limits manually is tedious. To differentiate efficiently, we use several established calculus rules.

\begin{theorembox}{Calculus Differentiation Rules}
Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable functions, and let $c$ be a constant.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constant Rule:} $\deriv{}{x}(c) = 0$
    \item \textbf{Power Rule:} $\deriv{}{x}(x^n) = n x^{n-1} \quad \forall n \in \R$
    \item \textbf{Sum/Difference Rule:} $\deriv{}{x}(f(x) \pm g(x)) = f'(x) \pm g'(x)$
    \item \textbf{Product Rule:} $\deriv{}{x}(f(x) \cdot g(x)) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)$
    \item \textbf{Quotient Rule:} $\deriv{}{x}\left(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right) = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{(g(x))^2}$
    \item \index{Chain Rule}\textbf{Chain Rule:} $\deriv{}{x}(f(g(x))) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$
\end{itemize}
\end{theorembox}

\textbf{Data Science Relevance (The Chain Rule):} When you train a Deep Neural Network, the output is a composite function of many layers: $L(W_3(W_2(W_1(X))))$. To find out how a weight $W_1$ in the very first layer affects the final loss $L$, the algorithm uses the \textbf{Chain Rule} to multiply the derivatives backward through the network. This is called \textbf{Backpropagation}.

\subsection{Derivatives of Standard Transcendental Functions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exponential:} $\deriv{}{x}(e^x) = e^x$
    \item \textbf{Logarithm:} $\deriv{}{x}(\ln(x)) = \frac{1}{x}$
\end{itemize}

\begin{examplebox}{Differentiating with Chain and Quotient Rules}
Find the derivative of $f(x) = \ln(x^2 + 3)$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Let the outer function be $u(y) = \ln(y)$ and the inner function be $y(x) = x^2 + 3$.
    \item Differentiate each component:
    $$u'(y) = \frac{1}{y}, \quad y'(x) = 2x$$
    \item Apply the Chain Rule:
    $$f'(x) = u'(y(x)) \cdot y'(x) = \frac{1}{x^2 + 3} \cdot (2x) = \frac{2x}{x^2 + 3}$$
\end{itemize}
\end{examplebox}

\section{L'Hôpital's Rule}
\index{L'Hôpital's Rule}

L'Hôpital's Rule provides an elegant method for evaluating limits that result in indeterminate forms (like $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$).

\begin{theorembox}{L'Hôpital's Rule}
If $\limit{x}{c} f(x) = 0$ and $\limit{x}{c} g(x) = 0$ (or if both limits are $\pm\infty$), then:
$$\limit{x}{c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \limit{x}{c} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$
provided the limit on the right exists (or is $\pm\infty$).
\end{theorembox}

\begin{examplebox}{Evaluating Indeterminate Limits}
Find the limit: $\limit{x}{0} \frac{e^{3x} - 1}{x}$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Direct substitution yields $\frac{e^0 - 1}{0} = \frac{1 - 1}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$, which is an indeterminate form.
    \item Apply L'Hôpital's Rule by differentiating the numerator and the denominator separately:
    $$\text{Numerator derivative: } \deriv{}{x}(e^{3x} - 1) = 3e^{3x}$$
    $$\text{Denominator derivative: } \deriv{}{x}(x) = 1$$
    \item Find the limit of the new quotient:
    $$\limit{x}{0} \frac{e^{3x} - 1}{x} = \limit{x}{0} \frac{3e^{3x}}{1} = 3e^0 = 3$$
\end{itemize}
\end{examplebox}

\section{Applications of Derivatives}

\subsection{Optimization: Maxima and Minima}
\index{Optimization}

In data science, we always want to find the minimum of a cost function (to reduce errors) or the maximum of a likelihood function (to increase accuracy). Derivatives allow us to find these extrema analytically.

\begin{definitionbox}{Critical Points and Extrema Tests}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Critical Point:} A value $c$ in the domain of $f$ where $f'(c) = 0$ (horizontal tangent) or $f'(c)$ is undefined.
    \item \textbf{First Derivative Test:} Let $c$ be a critical point of a continuous function $f$:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item If $f'(x)$ changes from positive (sloping up) to negative (sloping down) at $c$, then $f$ has a \textbf{local maximum} at $c$.
            \item If $f'(x)$ changes from negative to positive at $c$, then $f$ has a \textbf{local minimum} at $c$.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Second Derivative Test:} Suppose $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c)$ exists:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item If $f''(c) < 0$ (concave down), then $f$ has a \textbf{local maximum} at $c$.
            \item If $f''(c) > 0$ (concave up), then $f$ has a \textbf{local minimum} at $c$.
            \item If $f''(c) = 0$, the test is inconclusive (use the First Derivative Test).
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\textbf{Data Science Relevance (Gradient Descent):} Instead of solving $f'(x) = 0$ analytically (which is impossible for neural networks with billions of parameters), algorithms use \index{Gradient Descent}\textbf{Gradient Descent}. They compute the derivative at the current point, and take a small step in the \textit{opposite} direction of the slope, physically walking down the curve until they hit a minimum (where $f'(x) = 0$).

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Data Science: Gradient Descent Step in Python]
def f_prime(x):
    # Derivative of a simple loss function f(x) = x^2
    return 2 * x

x_current = 5.0  # Starting guess
learning_rate = 0.1

# Take one step down the gradient
step = learning_rate * f_prime(x_current)
x_new = x_current - step

print(f"Old x: {x_current}, New x: {x_new}")
# The value moved closer to 0 (the minimum of x^2)
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Common Pitfalls, Traps \& Misconceptions}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 1: Continuity Does NOT Guarantee Differentiability}
A classic error is assuming that every continuous function can be differentiated everywhere.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Kink/Corner Trap:} Consider the ReLU activation function $R(x) = \max(0, x)$. It is continuous everywhere on $\R$.
    \item However, at $x = 0$:
    $$\lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{R(0+h) - R(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{0}{h} = 0, \qquad \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{R(0+h) - R(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = 1$$
    Because the left-hand derivative ($0$) and right-hand derivative ($1$) do not match, $R'(0)$ \textbf{does not exist}.
    \item Machine learning frameworks resolve this at kinks by employing \textbf{subgradients} (setting $R'(0) = 0$ by convention).
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 2: The Quotient Rule Subtraction Order Trap}
When differentiating $\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}$, the numerator is $\mathbf{u'v - uv'}$, \textbf{never} $uv' - u'v$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reversing the order produces the exact negative of the true derivative: $\frac{uv' - u'v}{v^2} = -\deriv{}{x}\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)$.
    \item \textbf{Mnemonic:} "Low D-High minus High D-Low, over the square of what's below": $\frac{v \, du - u \, dv}{v^2}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 3: Misapplying L'Hôpital's Rule to Determinate Limits}
L'Hôpital's Rule is \textbf{strictly conditional}: it applies \textbf{only} when direct evaluation yields an indeterminate form: $\left[\frac{0}{0}\right]$ or $\left[\frac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}\right]$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Example:} Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x + 1}$.
    \item Direct substitution gives $\frac{2^2 + 1}{2 + 1} = \frac{5}{3}$ (a valid, determinate real number).
    \item \textbf{Incorrect:} Applying L'Hôpital's blindly: $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{2x}{1} = 4$ (Completely wrong!).
    \item Always verify the presence of an indeterminate form before differentiating!
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}{Trap 4: Inconclusive Second Derivative Test ($f''(c) = 0$)}
If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) = 0$, students frequently jump to the conclusion that $c$ is a point of inflection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $f''(c) = 0$ means the test is \textbf{inconclusive}; $c$ could be a local minimum, a local maximum, or an inflection point!
    \item \textbf{Example 1:} $f(x) = x^4$ at $x = 0$: $f'(0) = 0, f''(0) = 0$. But $x=0$ is a strict \textbf{global minimum}.
    \item \textbf{Example 2:} $f(x) = x^3$ at $x = 0$: $f'(0) = 0, f''(0) = 0$. Here $x=0$ is an \textbf{inflection point}.
    \item When $f''(c) = 0$, you \textbf{must} fall back to the First Derivative Test (checking the sign change of $f'(x)$ across $c$).
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\section{Python Implementation: Symbolic Derivatives, Hessians, and Gradient Descent}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Symbolic Calculus and Optimization in Python}]
import numpy as np
import sympy as sp

# 1. Symbolic Differentiation and Chain Rule Verification with SymPy
x, w = sp.symbols('x w', real=True)

# Deep Learning Composite: Loss L(w) = (sigmoid(2*w + 1) - 1)**2
sigmoid_expr = 1 / (1 + sp.exp(-(2*w + 1)))
loss_expr = (sigmoid_expr - 1)**2

grad_w = sp.diff(loss_expr, w)
hess_w = sp.diff(grad_w, w)

print(f"Loss Function:       L(w)  = {loss_expr}")
print(f"Analytical Gradient: dL/dw = {sp.simplify(grad_w)}")
print(f"Analytical Hessian:  d2L/dw2 = {sp.simplify(hess_w)}")

# Evaluate at w = 0.5
w_val = 0.5
print(f"Gradient at w=0.5:   {float(grad_w.subs(w, w_val)):.6f}")

# 2. Critical Point Analysis for Non-Linear Optimization
f_poly = x**3 - 6*x**2 + 9*x + 1
f_prime = sp.diff(f_poly, x)
f_double_prime = sp.diff(f_prime, x)

critical_pts = sp.solve(f_prime, x)
print(f"\nCritical Points of x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 1: {critical_pts}")
for pt in critical_pts:
    second_deriv_val = f_double_prime.subs(x, pt)
    nature = "Local Minimum (Concave Up)" if second_deriv_val > 0 else "Local Maximum (Concave Down)"
    print(f"  Point x = {pt} -> f''(x) = {second_deriv_val} -> {nature}")

# 3. L'Hopital's Rule Symbolic Verification
# Limit as x -> 0 of (e^(3x) - 1) / x
num = sp.exp(3*x) - 1
den = x
lim_val = sp.limit(num / den, x, 0)
print(f"\nL'Hopital Limit: lim_{{x->0}} (e^(3x) - 1)/x = {lim_val}")
\end{lstlisting}

    \item \textbf{Composite Chain Rule in Neural Backpropagation:} A machine learning model processes input $x$ through three sequential layers to produce loss $L$:
    $$u(x) = 3x - 1, \qquad v(u) = u^2 + 4, \qquad L(v) = \ln(v)$$
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Express $L$ directly as a single composite function of $x$: $L(x) = (L \circ v \circ u)(x)$.
        \item Compute the three individual derivatives: $\frac{dL}{dv}$, $\frac{dv}{du}$, and $\frac{du}{dx}$.
        \item Using the chain rule $\frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{dL}{dv} \cdot \frac{dv}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$, evaluate $\frac{dL}{dx}$ as an explicit rational function of $x$.
        \item Evaluate the gradient $\left.\frac{dL}{dx}\right|_{x = 1}$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \begin{enumerate}

        \item $\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln(x)}{x^2}$
        \item $\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^5 - 1}{x^3 - 1}$
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Extrema and Optimization of Non-Linear Functions:} Consider the polynomial cubic function:
    $$f(x) = 2x^3 - 9x^2 + 12x + 5$$
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Find the derivative $f'(x)$ and determine all critical points.
        \item Compute the second derivative $f''(x)$ and apply the Second Derivative Test to classify each critical point as a local minimum or local maximum.
        \item Find the point of inflection where concavity changes ($f''(x) = 0$).
        \item Find the absolute maximum and absolute minimum of $f(x)$ on the closed compact interval $[0, 3]$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Mean Squared Error Convex Optimization:} A machine learning cost function with respect to parameter vector scalar $w$ is defined by:
    $$J(w) = \frac{1}{2}(w - 5)^2 + \ln(w^2 + 1)$$
    \begin{enumerate}

\subsection*{Part B: Conceptual and Diagnostic Multiple Choice Questions}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: Continuity vs. Differentiability Kink Fallacy} \\
Let $f(x) = |x - 3|$ be defined on $\R$. An engineering student asserts: \textit{"Because $f(x)$ is continuous everywhere and $f(3) = 0$, its derivative at $x = 3$ is $f'(3) = 0$."} What is the \textbf{correct mathematical refutation}?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The student is correct; the minimum of any continuous curve always has a derivative of zero.
    \item The student is incorrect because the left-hand derivative is $\lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{|h|}{h} = -1$, while the right-hand derivative is $\lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = +1$; because the one-sided limits differ, $f'(3)$ does not exist (a sharp corner / non-differentiable point).
    \item The student is incorrect because $f(x)$ is not continuous at $x = 3$.
    \item The student is incorrect because the derivative is undefined everywhere on the real line.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Conceptual Diagnostic: L'Hôpital's Rule Precondition Violation} \\
A student is asked to evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos(x)}{x + 1}$. The student applies L'Hôpital's Rule:
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos(x)}{x + 1} \stackrel{\text{L'H}}{=} \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{-\sin(x)}{1} = -\sin(0) = 0$$
What is the \textbf{critical mathematical error} in the student's solution?
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The derivative of $\cos(x)$ is $+\sin(x)$, not $-\sin(x)$.
    \item The denominator derivative is $x$, not $1$.
    \item Direct substitution yields $\frac{\cos(0)}{0 + 1} = \frac{1}{1} = 1.0$, which is determinate; L'Hôpital's Rule was applied illegally to a non-indeterminate expression, producing a false result.
    \item L'Hôpital's Rule can only be applied to polynomials.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
